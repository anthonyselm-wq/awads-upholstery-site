Automotive Upholstery From a Shop That's Seen Every Era of Car

Awad's Upholstery opened in 1960, which means we've been repairing car and truck interiors since the muscle car era, through the decades of vinyl bench seats, into the leather-heavy interiors of today, and now the mix of cloth, leather, and synthetic materials that come through daily-driver and work-truck interiors alike. Whatever decade your vehicle is from, chances are we've worked on something similar — and if we haven't, we'll figure out the right approach before we ever cut a piece of material.

Automotive interiors take damage differently than marine or commercial upholstery does. Ohio's temperature swings — brutal summer sun baking a dashboard one month, freezing cold making vinyl and leather brittle the next — are hard on interior materials in a way that milder climates don't experience. Add in daily wear from getting in and out, kids, pets, and years of sun through the windshield, and most interiors eventually need some combination of new seat covers, foam, or trim work.

Signs Your Interior Needs Attention

Most of the vehicles that come through our shop show a handful of these issues:

  • Cracked or split seams on seat bolsters, especially the driver's seat
  • Foam that's compressed flat, leaving seats feeling like the springs are showing through
  • A headliner sagging down from the ceiling, sometimes touching your head while driving
  • Door panels that are torn, faded, or coming loose from the door frame
  • Sun-cracked dash pads or dash caps
  • Vinyl or leather that's stiff, brittle, or peeling from age and temperature swings
  • An interior that doesn't match the effort you've put into the exterior of a restoration

Our Automotive Upholstery Process

Every job starts the same way, whether it's a work truck seat cover or a full classic car interior restoration:

  1. Free estimate and inspection. Bring the vehicle by so we can look at the seats, headliner, panels, or carpet in question and talk through what's actually needed.
  2. Material and pattern selection. We'll help you choose cloth, vinyl, or leather, and match factory stitch patterns as closely as possible on restorations, or work from your preferences on a custom job.
  3. Foam and padding replacement. Compressed or broken-down foam is replaced so the seat doesn't just look new — it sits right, too.
  4. Cutting and stitching in-house. Every panel is measured, cut, and sewn in our shop, not outsourced to a supplier who's never seen your vehicle.
  5. Installation and fit check. Seats, panels, and trim go back in with attention to how they seat against the frame, hardware, and any electronics like seat heaters that need to be reinstalled with care.

Materials We Work With

Vinyl & Leather

We work with automotive-grade vinyl and leather in a wide range of colors and grains, matched to factory originals where possible for restorations, or chosen fresh for a custom look. Both materials are more resistant to daily wear than cloth, which is why they're common choices for work vehicles and daily drivers alike.

Cloth & Original-Pattern Fabrics

For interiors where cloth is the right call — either for comfort, cost, or matching a factory-original look on a classic restoration — we source fabric that matches period-correct patterns as closely as possible, so a restored bench seat doesn't look out of place next to the rest of the car.

Headliner Material & Backing

Sagging headliners are almost always a backing failure, not a fabric failure — the foam backing that holds the fabric to the headboard breaks down with age and heat. We replace the backing along with fresh headliner material so the repair holds instead of sagging again in a year or two.

Classic Car & Restoration Work

Restorations get special attention because the goal isn't just a comfortable seat — it's an interior that looks correct for the year and model. That means matching stitch spacing, panel shapes, and material texture to what left the factory, not just picking a similar-looking vinyl off the shelf. If you're restoring a car for a show, a sale, or just to enjoy the way it was built, we'll talk through how period-correct you want to go before any material gets cut.

Some owners want an exact factory match down to the stitch count; others are happy with a period-appropriate look that doesn't have to be museum-precise. Both approaches are common, and neither is wrong — it comes down to whether the car is headed for judged shows, a sale where originality matters to the buyer, or just years of enjoyable weekend driving. We'll ask about your goals for the car before recommending an approach, rather than assuming everyone wants the same level of detail.

Fleet & Work Truck Seat Repair

Work trucks and fleet vehicles put more hours and more wear into a seat in a single year than most personal vehicles see in five. Tool belts, work boots, and daily in-and-out use break down bolsters and seams fast, and a torn seat on a work truck is more than a cosmetic problem — it's a comfort and professionalism issue for a vehicle that might be showing up at a customer's house. We handle single-vehicle repairs as well as multi-vehicle fleet work, and durable vinyl is usually the right call for these interiors given how much abuse they take.

Caring for Your Interior Between Repairs

Parking in shade or using a windshield sunshade cuts down on the UV exposure that cracks vinyl and dashboards fastest. Cleaning leather and vinyl with products made for those materials, rather than generic household cleaners, helps keep them flexible instead of drying them out. And addressing a small tear or a loose seam early — before it's exposed to more flexing and weather — almost always costs less than waiting until the damage spreads. Conditioning leather a few times a year and keeping cloth seats vacuumed to avoid ground-in grit both go a long way toward stretching the life of an interior between full reupholstery jobs.

Common Questions

Automotive Upholstery FAQ

Leather, vinyl, or cloth — which should I choose?

It depends on your budget, how the vehicle is used, and whether you're matching an original interior. Leather and vinyl hold up better to daily wear and are easier to clean; cloth is often the right call for a period-correct restoration. We'll talk through the trade-offs at your estimate.

Can you save a seat that's torn but not fully destroyed?

Often, yes. A single torn seam or panel can frequently be repaired without redoing the whole seat, as long as the surrounding material and foam are still in good shape. We'll let you know honestly whether a repair or full reupholstery makes more sense.

How do you fix a sagging headliner?

We remove the headliner panel, strip the old fabric and failed foam backing, and install new headliner material with fresh backing so it holds its shape against the headboard instead of sagging again.

Can you match the stitching pattern on a classic car interior?

Matching factory stitch spacing and panel shapes is a normal part of restoration work for us. Bring in photos or the original material if you have it, and we'll work to get as close to period-correct as possible.

Do you work with insurance claims for interior damage?

We can provide a written estimate you can submit to your insurance company for interior damage from an accident or other covered event. Bring in any claim paperwork you have and we'll work with what you need.

How long does a full interior restoration take?

A single seat repair might be quick, while a full interior — seats, headliner, door panels, and carpet — takes longer, especially on a restoration where pattern matching is involved. We'll give you a realistic timeline at your free estimate.

Can you reinstall seat heaters and other electronics?

Yes. When a seat has built-in heating elements or other wiring, we take care during teardown and reinstallation so those components go back in working order along with the new upholstery.
